What is this?

How far the ball travels each frame, in field units (the field is 800 wide). Range 2 to 10, default 5. Every paddle hit adds 3% up to double the base speed, and each serve starts over. The fly's reaction time is fixed by its wiring, about 10 brain milliseconds from seeing the ball to a descending command, so a faster ball gives it less time to line up.

Bot
What is this?

Hands your paddle to a script. The bot follows the ball at a capped 4 units per frame while the ball is coming toward it, and drifts back to center otherwise. It is deliberately beatable: a steep angle off the fly's paddle can outrun it. Toggle with the checkbox or the B key, even mid-rally. Leave it on to train the fly unattended.

What is this?

The connectome has 5,895 photoreceptors, each wired to a column of the optic lobe with a known hexagonal position. The game maps the field onto those columns: distance from the paddle runs along one hex axis, distance from the paddle's height along the other, so the ball's position picks one column in one eye. Every column gets the light level; the columns within the ball radius get it taken away, because a real fly's escape circuit answers to a dark spot growing on the eye. The looming bar is the optional shortcut that drives the LC4 and LPLC2 looming detectors of that eye directly, scaled by how close the ball is, how far off center, and whether it is approaching. The mushroom-body bar is the same shape sent to the visual projection neurons that feed the Kenyon cells, the memory that dopamine can rewrite. The spike counts on the line above are what those cells actually did this tick.

What makes it want to hit the ball?

Nothing in a wiring diagram wants anything, so the game gives the fly what a real fly wants. A return puts sugar on its mouth: its 275 labellar taste neurons fire for 300 ms (the data set does not tell sugar cells from bitter ones, so all of them). A miss puts heat on its antennae: its 7 hot cells fire for 300 ms, the punishment used in the classic flight-simulator experiments where tethered flies learn to steer away from heat. With Dopamine injection on, the reward and punishment dopamine cells (PAM and PPL1) are also driven directly, scaled by how surprising the outcome was; off, only the fly's own taste and heat circuits can reach them. The learning window is now 1.5 s, the mushroom body's real pairing time, so the paddle move that caused the outcome is still eligible when the dopamine arrives; in the first three training sessions it was 100 ms and the credit was gone before the reward came. Turn on the bot and leave learning on to train it unattended; uv run python -m flypong.train runs the same experiment headless and prints the numbers.

What is this?

Every time the fly returns the ball, its 316 PAM dopamine neurons are stimulated; every time it misses, its 16 PPL1 dopamine neurons are. These are the cells a real fly uses to signal reward and punishment. The strength of each burst is the prediction error: how surprising the outcome was given the fly's recent return rate, so an expected return barely registers and a miss after a long streak is a big signal. Their spikes travel down their real axons, rebuilt from the raw synapse counts, to the 8,599 neurons they actually innervate: almost every Kenyon cell and output neuron of the mushroom body, the fly's learning center, plus a few dozen descending neurons.

Meanwhile 4.36 million synapses can change. Every neuron keeps a presynaptic trace that rises when its spikes transmit and fades over 20 ms; when a neuron fires, each plastic synapse onto it gains its sender's trace as eligibility, which fades over 100 ms. Each tick, eligible synapses that received dopamine change: reward strengthens them, punishment weakens them, scaled by the learning rate. A synapse never changes sign and stays between a tenth and four times its original strength.

Two things are ours, not the fly's. A weak diffuse reward signal also reaches the reflex arc that drives the paddle, the 237,432 synapses leaving the looming detectors or landing on the escape neurons, because in this model mushroom-body output cannot reach the escape neurons on its own. An earlier version spread that reward over the 1.2 million visual-to-descending synapses and learned to play worse because it blurred the left-right signal. Punishment reaches the arc only if you tick the box; with it on, the first version of this page learned to play worse for a different reason, every miss weakened the reflex that had just fired. And reward potentiates while punishment depresses, where the real mushroom body depresses in both cases with the valence set by which output neuron is affected.

Learned weights are saved every minute and on shutdown, and reloaded when the server starts. Forget everything restores the original connectome. The fly cannot learn a new direction of movement, that is fixed by its anatomy; what it can learn is how fast and how hard to react.

What is this?

How many brain milliseconds pass for each game frame. Range 1 to 16, default 4. The status line shows how many steps per second this Mac reaches; at 1 ms steps the brain runs faster than real time when idle and around 400 steps per second during play, so 4 gives about 20 to 25 brain updates per second. More brain time per frame sharpens reactions but lowers the update rate.

What is this?

The integration time step of the neuron model. The published model uses 0.1 ms; this page offers 1, 0.5 and 0.25 ms. The membrane and synapse equations are integrated exactly at any step, so the step mostly changes how finely the 1.8 ms transmission delay and 2.2 ms refractory period are resolved, at the cost of proportionally more compute per brain millisecond.

What is this?

Random membrane current added to every neuron each step, in millivolts per square-root millisecond. A real fly brain is never silent; this is the only source of spontaneous activity here. Measured on this Mac: at 0.5 the brain idles nearly silent (0.01 Hz); at 0.6 to 0.8 it idles at 1 to 1.5 Hz; at 1.0 at about 3 Hz, all realistic. But the escape readout that moves the paddle drowns in that background: the left-right separation is 2.7 to 1 at zero noise, 1.9 to 1 at 0.5, and gone by 1.0. Default 0.5 keeps the fly playable; raise it to see a brain that idles like a real one and plays worse.

What is this?

Short-term synaptic depression: the fraction of a neuron's transmitter each spike uses up, recovering over 300 ms. Real synapses do this. It is not in the published model, and without it this network is supercritical: any sustained input ignites a self-sustained state where the whole brain fires at 10 Hz, including the Kenyon cells that should be almost silent. At 0.2 activity always dies back down when input stops. At 0 you can watch the ignition happen.

What is this?

The drive given to the looming detectors on the eye facing the ball: 126 LC4 cells and 185 LPLC2 cells, the neurons a real fly uses to notice something rushing at it. Scaled by how close the ball is and how far it sits above or below the paddle, and cut to a quarter when the ball is moving away. Range 0 to 1, default 0.3. This is the shortcut that actually moves the paddle: in a real fly the retina would drive these cells, but in this model it cannot (see the light setting).

What is this?

Untick to make the fly rely on its real eye alone. Measured result: it goes blind. With the shortcut off, the escape neurons fire equally for a ball above or below in every light setting, and the looming detectors never fire from retinal input. The lamina neurons of a real fly are non-spiking graded cells, and a spiking model cannot carry a small patch of retina through them to the lobula. The switch is here so you can confirm it yourself.

What is this?

Constant drive on the 5,895 photoreceptors that have an eye column (inherited from the lamina cell each one synapses on most; their own cell bodies are in the retina, outside the imaged volume). The ball is painted as a dark spot in the columns it covers, which is the stimulus a real fly's escape circuit responds to. Watch the retina flare in the optic lobe of the brain view. Range 0 to 1, default 0.15; higher values make thousands of photoreceptors fire at their maximum rate and flood the brain with activity without changing play.

What is this?

How much darker the ball's columns are than the lit background: 1 is a black ball, 0 makes the ball invisible to the retina. Only affects the photoreceptor pattern, since the retinal pathway does not reach the paddle in this model.

What is this?

Drive on the 252 visual projection neurons that synapse onto the fly's Kenyon cells, on the eye facing the ball, with the same shape as the looming term. This is a real pathway into the fly's learning center, so the mushroom body sees the ball and its synapses can change with dopamine. Whether that changes play is another matter: mushroom-body output does not reach the escape neurons in this model. The activity line shows Kenyon-cell and output-neuron spikes.

What is this?

Normally the paddle follows the fly's escape descending neurons (the 320 DNp cells, 160 per side). Tick this to follow the nerve cord's 708 real leg and wing motor neurons instead, one hop closer to a body. Measured result: motor-neuron activity is nearly the same on both sides for a ball above or below, because turning uses both sides of the body, so as a single up-or-down number it barely works. A real body would need the full pattern, which is out of scope here.

What is this?

Divides the left-minus-right escape count by a slow running average of the total escape activity (about 50 ticks) before it becomes a paddle command. A brain that gets globally more excitable, for instance because learning strengthened many synapses at once, then produces the same command instead of a noisier, stronger one. Measured motivation: in the first learning session on this model, reward spread over 1.2 million synapses and blurred the left-right signal. Untick to use the raw count difference.

What is this?

Each tick the server counts escape-neuron spikes on the left and right, takes right minus left, and smooths that with a leaky sum that keeps 70% of its value from tick to tick. Motor gain multiplies the smoothed value into a paddle command between -1 (full speed up) and 1 (full speed down). Range 0 to 5, default 0.5. Low values make the fly sluggish; high values make it twitchy and prone to overshooting the ball.

Real: 166,700 neurons and 25.6 million synapses from the MaleCNS v1.0 connectome, run with the published Shiu et al. 2024 neuron model (resting -52 mV, threshold -45 mV, 0.275 mV per synapse, 5 ms synapses, 1.8 ms delays, 2.2 ms refractory period), with histamine, the photoreceptor transmitter, treated as the fast inhibitory transmitter it is, stepping live and learning through its own dopamine neurons. Ours: inhibitory synapses scaled by 2 (at the published 1x the mushroom body ignites, with Kenyon cells at 21 Hz each instead of below 1 Hz, and the escape signal blurs), short-term synaptic depression to keep the network from igniting, the looming shortcut because the retinal pathway cannot reach the escape neurons in this model, the diffuse reward signal on the paddle pathway, and the up-or-down readout.
